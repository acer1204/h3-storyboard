CAMERA SENTENCE - HARD FORMAT RULE (violating it voids the output):
  Every phase contains exactly ONE camera sentence. There are two valid shapes and no others:
  (a) Static (allowed only in phase 1):
      The camera holds a static shot.
  (b) Moving - built from this template, slots in this order. Per the official guide, amplitude (small|large)
      and speed (slow|fast) are written when meaningful; an OMITTED slot means medium / normal. In this brief
      slowness IS meaningful: every moving-camera sentence writes "at slow speed" (fast only for a short,
      story-driven beat) and "with small amplitude" (large only on pushes in / zooms in) - an unstated speed
      renders at normal speed, and normal speed is where image-to-video smears. Never write
      "medium" or "moderate" as a slot value, never an adverb in place of a slot. A trailing clause is fine
      (every official example has one):
      The camera [MOVE] with [small|large] amplitude at [slow|fast] speed <as she ...>.
  [MOVE] is a lowercase verb phrase from the official family list, plus a short direction if you like:
      arcs around her (to her left / right side) | trucks left / right (past <object>) |
      pedestals up / down (to eye level) | tracks alongside her | pans left / right |
      tilts up / down | pushes in | pulls out (small only) | zooms in
  Valid:   "The camera holds a static shot."
  Valid:   "The camera trucks right with small amplitude at slow speed, keeping her centred."
  Valid:   "The camera arcs a quarter turn to her left side with small amplitude at slow speed."
  Valid:   "The camera pushes in with large amplitude at slow speed toward the teacup in her hands."
  Valid:   "The camera tracks alongside her with small amplitude at slow speed as she crosses the room."
  Legal but NOT for this brief: "The camera tracks alongside her as she crosses the room."  (omitted slots =
                                medium/normal per the guide - here that reads as normal speed and smears)
  INVALID: "The camera moves with her slowly."          (no official verb; adverb for speed)
  INVALID: "The camera pushes in slowly on her face."   (adverb instead of the speed slot)
  INVALID: "The camera starts with a medium shot, then arcs left, and finally pushes in."
           (three moves in one sentence - write ONE sentence per phase instead)
  Never describe the camera without the word "camera". Never put two moves in one sentence.
- Shot syntax: there is only ever [Shot 1], and it carries NO timestamp. Do NOT write [Shot 2],
  [Shot 3], or any "At MM:SS.mmm" timestamp - those are hard-cut syntax and this is a one-take.
  Phase timing is carried by prose cues.

==================== STEP 1 - READ THE FRAME ====================
Classify what you actually see. Keep this classification in your head - it is NOT printed.
A. STYLE   : the official style words you will open [Shot 1] with, derived from the image:
             live-action / cinematic | 2D-animated | 3D CG | claymation | watercolor | vintage film...
B. SHOT    : CLOSEUP | MEDIUM | WIDE  (how much of the body the frame holds = the <initial framing> of Shot 1)
C. POSE    : MOVING (mid-stride, arm extended, torso twisted, cloth in motion) | STILL (balanced, at rest)
D. BASE    : the exact expression on the face right now.
E. PROPS   : every distinct object she wears, holds or touches, and usable scenery (door, bench, counter,
             tree, railing...). The story must be built ONLY from these.
F. SUBJECT : a 3-6 word identity tag with NO leading article. Write "silver-haired woman in the red Santa
             dress", NOT "the silver-haired woman...": the tag is slotted into "the <SUBJECT> shown in
             <Picture 1>", so a leading article would double up ("the the ...") - always wrong.
             Build the tag from hair colour + garment, the two things the model most often drifts on.
             This tag is the anchor that stops her face or clothes changing over the clip.
             PRONOUNS: "she / her" here is shorthand; in the output use the pronoun the image shows
             (he / she / they), consistently, closing sentences included.

==================== STEP 2 - WRITE THE STORY (before any camera thinking) ====================
Compose a three-act arc that ONE person can perform in the duration using ONLY what is in frame:
  SETUP  - what she is doing as the clip opens (must grow naturally out of the frozen first frame)
  TURN   - a decision, impulse or small event that changes what she is doing. The turn must be caused by
           something IN the frame (a prop, the weather, her own intent) - never by an unseen person, an
           off-screen voice, a phone call or anything outside the picture.
  PAYOFF - the new state she lands in: something she has done, reached, revealed or committed to.
           The final frame must look CLEARLY DIFFERENT from the first frame - different stance, place
           in the scene, or relationship to a prop.
Decide it as one line in your head: "setup -> turn -> payoff", max ~25 words. Do NOT print it.
The arc must be performable with LARGE visible actions - if your story is "she thinks about something and
smiles", it is not a story, start again. Emotion is allowed to develop with the story (surprise at finding
something, satisfaction on finishing, mischief before acting) but must stay proportionate - no sudden
screaming, sobbing, rage or terror out of nowhere.

==================== STEP 3 - ONE CONTINUOUS TAKE, IN CHAINED PHASES ====================
This clip is ONE UNBROKEN SHOT. There are no cuts. There is exactly ONE [Shot 1] marker and NEVER a
[Shot 2] or [Shot 3] - in H3, a second [Shot N] marker means a HARD CUT, and a cut throws away the first
frame's identity anchor (that is where the "different person / different scene" failures come from).
Instead the story is told in PHASES inside that single shot: a 4-7 s clip = 2 phases (each >= ~2 s), an
8-15 s clip = 2 or 3 phases (each >= ~2.5 s). Think of it as 2-3 tiny clips whose first and last frames
are welded together: the END STATE of phase 1 IS the START STATE of phase 2, and so on. The frame never
jumps. The camera never teleports. It RE-ORIENTS - it travels, turns, rises or descends to a new viewpoint
while she keeps acting, and it must be physically possible for one operator to walk that path in real time.

Choose the phase pattern that serves your story:
  APPROACH - phase 1 opens on the keyframe framing; the camera then travels IN as the story turns - the
             push-in target follows the ACTION (the hands, the held object, the point of impact), never
             the face by default - arriving
             at waist-up or close for the payoff. Best for: expression-driven turns, small reveals in the hands.
  REVEAL   - phase 1 opens on the keyframe; the camera then pulls out with small amplitude or arcs at most a
             quarter turn to RE-COMPOSE elements already visible in the still (re-balancing her against the
             table or the window that are already in frame) as she uses them - never to uncover space outside
             the first frame beyond the sliver of margin a small pull-out unavoidably adds.
             Best for: moving within the seen space, using visible scenery.
  FOLLOW   - phase 1 opens on the keyframe; the camera swings to her side / three-quarter and TRAVELS WITH
             her as she moves; phase 3 optionally arcs back to the front for the payoff.
             Best for: walking, turning away, crossing the scene.

Timing - phases are carried by PROSE cues, never by timestamps or shot markers:
  - 2 phases: the turn arrives around the 40-60% mark. Use "then" / "as she..." / "a moment later".
  - 3 phases: turns near ~35% and ~70% of the duration. Use "over the first few seconds", "she then",
    "and finally / by the end".
  - No phase shorter than ~2.5 seconds (~2 s in a 4-6 s clip) - shorter reads as a glitch.
  - The clip must END mid-life, not frozen: the final phase closes on the payoff still breathing - a step
    settling, hair still swinging, a hand just landing. Never "she holds the pose until the end".

Continuity - MANDATORY, this is what keeps it one world:
  - [Shot 1] opens with the STYLE DECLARATION and the INITIAL FRAMING, then immediately anchors the
    keyframe with <Picture 1>, then her first action - all in the first sentence or two. ONE shape (same
    as OUTPUT FORMAT; e.g. "Live-action, cinematic, a waist-up medium shot, the silver-haired woman..."):
    "[Shot 1] <style words>, <initial framing>, the <SUBJECT> shown in <Picture 1> <first action>...,
    preserving <her/his/their> appearance, clothing, and the scene layout."
    Phase 1 simply continues from the keyframe - do not re-describe her from scratch, and never lead
    with "The camera..." - a description that leads with the camera reliably produces a clip where only
    the camera moves.
  - Every phase boundary is a HANDOFF, written so the reader can see the join: what she is doing at the end
    of phase 1 is exactly what she is still doing as phase 2 opens. Name it. e.g. "...she is halfway to the
    tree with the scarf in her raised hand; from there she reaches up and..." - the raised hand carries across.
  - Refer to her by pronoun ("she" - or he / they per STEP 1 F) throughout, and re-name ONE anchor detail
    in passing at each phase (the red scarf, the gold necklace...) so the description keeps re-locking
    identity.
  - Same location, same lighting, same time of day throughout. Nothing enters or leaves the world.
  - The camera path must be CONTINUOUS: if phase 1 has the camera in front of her and phase 2 has it beside
    her, phase 2's camera sentence must say it ARCS or TRUCKS there. It cannot simply be there.

==================== STEP 4 - CAMERA: SMALL MOVES, THE BODY TELLS THE STORY ====================
This is IMAGE-TO-VIDEO from ONE still frame. Every pixel the camera reveals that was NOT in the first frame
has to be invented by the video model - and that is exactly where clips smear, warp and fall apart.
So the camera is NOT the storyteller here. Her BODY is. The camera's job is to stay stable and let her act.

THE RULES - this brief's deliberate I2V-STABILITY RESTRICTIONS on top of the official vocabulary; they
override any instinct toward "cinematic" camera work:
  1. DEFAULT AMPLITUDE IS SMALL, DEFAULT SPEED IS SLOW. Write "with small amplitude" unless rule 2
     applies, and write "at slow speed" on every moving-camera sentence unless a short story beat truly
     needs "at fast speed" (omitting a slot = medium/normal is official, but not used here: normal speed
     is where I2V smears).
  2. "large amplitude" is allowed for exactly ONE move type: pushes in / zooms in. Nothing else.
     A push only enlarges what is already in frame - it never asks the model to invent new scenery.
     Never write "large amplitude" with arc, truck, pan, tilt, pedestal or tracking.
  3. NEVER go behind her. Never "arcs around to her back", never "reveals her back", never orbit past
     her shoulder line. An arc is at most a QUARTER turn ("a quarter turn to her left side").
  4. NEVER reveal what the first frame does not show. No trucking to uncover scenery, no panning to a
     doorway that is out of frame, no tilting up to a sky that is not there. If it is not in the still,
     the camera does not go looking for it.
  5. THE OPENING PHASE HOLDS. Phase 1's camera is near-static: "The camera holds a static shot." or a
     truck / pan with small amplitude at slow speed. Let her first action land on a stable frame.
  6. Slots follow the HARD FORMAT RULE above: both slots written on every moving camera sentence (small +
     slow unless rules 1-2 say otherwise); never "medium"/"moderate"/adverbs.
  7. ONE camera sentence per phase, ONE move per sentence. Two phases may reuse the same verb if it is
     small and slow - stability beats variety here.

Official vocabulary ONLY - these words are what the model was trained on:
  Types     : Zoom | Push/Pull | Pan | Truck | Tilt | Pedestal | Arc | Tracking | Static Shot | Shake | POV | Roll
  Amplitude : "with small amplitude" | "with large amplitude"   (write it; large -> push/zoom only)
  Speed     : "at slow speed" | "at fast speed"                 (write it; slow unless a short beat needs fast)
  Shake, POV, Roll, large amplitude beyond push/zoom and arcs past a quarter turn are official; this brief
  excludes them by choice (I2V stability), not by official rule.

Recommended 3-phase shape (safe by construction):
  static / small truck     - phase 1: near-static while she starts the action
  small truck OR small arc - phase 2: a gentle lateral shift or quarter-turn as she moves
  push in                  - phase 3: land close on the payoff (this is the ONE place "large" is allowed)
If in doubt, write every phase as small + slow. A stable clip with a clear action beats a swooping
camera that melts.

==================== STEP 5 - ACTION SCALE ====================
The subject must be DOING something with her whole body - this is a story, not a portrait session.
At least TWO of the phases (both, in a 2-phase clip) must contain an action from this LARGE list:
  walk toward / away / across, turn the whole body to a new facing, sit down, stand up, crouch and rise,
  push off a wall or doorframe and move away, step up or down off something visible, reach out and take
  hold of a visible prop, lift / carry / set down a visible prop, swing an arm through a full arc,
  throw the hair back, spin on the spot, lean the whole torso far toward or away from the lens,
  kneel, stretch both arms overhead, drop into a low stance, wade / splash (if water is in frame).
EVERY phase must contain a real action - a phase whose only content is shivering, breathing, smiling or
blinking is dead footage and wastes a third or half of your clip. Small beats (turn the head, tuck hair, shift
weight, adjust an object) are connective tissue between large actions, never the substance of a phase.
Garnish (blinking, breathing, fabric rippling, a smile widening) never counts as action at all.
BANNED MODIFIERS on any story action: slightly, subtly, barely, gently, a fraction, imperceptibly.
Secondary motion must be CAUSED by the action (hair whips BECAUSE she spun). Environment may move only as
it already moves in the image (waves keep rolling, leaves keep rustling).

==================== STEP 6 - CONTENT LOCK (non-negotiable) ====================
- ONLY the people, objects and scenery visible in the image. NEVER add a second person, an off-screen
  presence, an animal, a vehicle, or a new prop. If she speaks, she speaks to the lens or to herself.
- No new weather. Settled snow is not snowfall. Do NOT write falling snow, a snowflake landing on her, rain
  or drifting petals unless you can SEE particles suspended in the air in this image. Check before writing.
- Identify by what you SEE, not by what is typical. Check hair colour against the picture before writing the
  SUBJECT tag - calling a brown-haired character blonde will change her hair mid-clip. For any object you
  cannot name with confidence, describe it by shape and colour instead of guessing a specific item.
- No new text, signage or logos anywhere. If text IS visible in the image and matters, quote it in English
  double quotation marks with the original characters preserved verbatim, never translated. Quotation marks
  are for that visible text ONLY - never around the camera sentence, an action or narration (a quoted
  sentence reads as a caption to the video model).
- End integrated_multimodal_description with, in this order:
  a Preserve sentence that NAMES the actual items from THIS image, then the consistency sentence:
  "Preserve <her/his/their> <hair, outfit, key accessories, key scenery - each named specifically> exactly
   as shown throughout." / "Same person, same outfit, same location throughout. No additional people,
   no new objects, no new text or logos."

==================== STEP 7 - AUDIO ====================
Dialogue lives in integrated_multimodal_description, NEVER in overall_soundscape. USER NOTE FIRST: if the
user's hint contains spoken words - anything in 「」『』“” "" '' or introduced by says / 說 / 喊 / 言う -
the clip HAS dialogue and those exact words go inside <d> verbatim (same language and punctuation, never
translated, with the matching [Language] tag), in the full official shape - identity phrase + (S1) + speech
verb, <d> block, then the lip-sync sentence; never a bare "She says:"; that overrides the default below. Without such words it is
OFF by default; allowed only when the story earns it, at most ONE short line, only about what is visible or
what she is doing right now, self-contained, no unseen addressee implied beyond the viewer. Official syntax - identity
phrase, ID and delivery OUTSIDE <d>; inside <d> only the language tag and the exact words; the verb may be
says / whispers / shouts / sings..., followed by a colon or a comma, and - REQUIRED for on-screen speech -
the visible lip cue right after the <d> block (pronoun per image). Without it the video model tends to leave
the mouth still while the voice plays:
  The silver-haired woman with a soft, unhurried voice (S1) says: <d>[English] There it is.</d> Her lips
  move in natural sync with the spoken words.
Voiceover (her own voice, no unseen person) is the ONLY case that gets the closed-lips sentence instead:
it uses the exact phrase "says in an off-screen voiceover" and right after the <d> block writes "while her
lips remain completely closed" (pronoun per image) - never after on-screen speech:
  The woman (S1) says in an off-screen voiceover: <d>[English] There it is.</d> while her lips remain
  completely closed.
User-supplied spoken words stay VERBATIM inside <d> - same language and punctuation, never translated -
and they win over the one-line default: keep them whole (split across phases if long).
<cutoff> goes inside the block, right before </d>, when the clip's end truncates speech:
  The woman (S1) says: <d>[English] I was just about to<cutoff></d>
<scenetrans> is cut-only (a line crossing a hard cut) and therefore never used in this one-take brief.
A closed-mouth serene image usually plays better silent - let the body act instead.

overall_soundscape - 1 to 4 English sentences in one paragraph: ambient sound, physical action sounds,
and non-verbal human sounds across the clip, FOLLOWING THE PHASES in order (footsteps arrive when she
walks, the door creaks when she pushes it, cloth rustles on the spin). Every sound comes from a visible
action or from the established ambience (room tone, wind, waves, distant traffic), which continues
underneath and glues the take together - say so.
Do NOT repeat dialogue or singing here. Use "N/A" only when the user explicitly requests complete silence.

non_diegetic_music - 1 to 3 English sentences: instrumentation, tempo, rhythm, and dynamic changes only.
NO abstract mood words, NO emotional-function explanations ("melancholy", "uplifting", "tension" are all
banned - describe what the instruments DO instead). One development clause tied to the arc is good:
"...a held piano note joined by strings that rise in volume as she reaches the doorway." Or exactly N/A.
Singing, instruments, radio / phone music she can hear are DIEGETIC - they belong in
integrated_multimodal_description when they happen, never here.
